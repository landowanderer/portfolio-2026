An AI assistant for finding the right ADU.

Choosing an accessory dwelling unit (ADU) means comparing layouts, understanding costs and checking what a property can accommodate.

I led the UX and visual design of an assistant for LiveLarge’s website. It helps visitors explain their needs, compare suitable models and move toward a lot review, while keeping their preferences in context as they browse.

01 Reframe

Help people work through a major decision.

An accessory dwelling unit (ADU) brings together questions that the original site answered on separate pages: which home fits, what the project may cost, and what the property allows. I reframed the assistant as a guide through those connected decisions—carrying a visitor’s priorities across the site, making trade-offs legible, and handing property-specific answers to LiveLarge’s team.

The site I started with

These are LiveLarge’s original pages, before the AI assistant. I reviewed the route from browsing a home to checking a property, looking at what already worked and where a conversation could help connect the details.

The challenge I took forward

My challenge was to help visitors work through questions about models, costs, and property requirements while keeping the homepage’s product content in focus. I designed the conversation to follow their browsing, retaining their needs as they moved into model details and property questions.

02 Page and conversation context

Keep the conversation while the page changes.

“What about this one?” only makes sense if the assistant knows which home is on screen. The challenge was to follow the page without losing a visitor’s earlier needs. I kept the current model visible beneath the chat title and carried the conversation across pages, so each new view has the right reference and the household’s brief stays available.

  1. 2.2

    Make the page reference visible.

    I placed the model name below the chat title. A visitor can see which home the answer refers to before reading the reply.

  2. 2.3

    Change the reference, keep the brief.

    I let the viewing label follow the model on screen without rewriting earlier replies. Watch the page scroll to L-795: the reference changes, while the two-bedroom preference and budget question stay in the thread.

03 Clarify and recommend

Ask only what changes the answer.

A rental goal leaves several decisions open: upfront cost, living space and what can fit on the property. I made clarification depend on what is missing from the conversation. In this path, the assistant asks for a priority, then the bedroom count, before comparing relevant models.

  1. 3.2

    Ask what matters before choosing a model.

    “Rental income” can mean keeping the initial cost down or making room for more people. I surfaced those priorities as a small choice, instead of treating the goal as enough to recommend a home.

  2. 3.3

    Ask the question that changes the shortlist.

    After “Lower upfront cost,” bedroom count is still missing. I ask for it here because it changes which models belong in the comparison. A known preference should not be asked again.

  3. Let’s keep two bedrooms in the mix! L-795 starts $20k below L-800, with two bedrooms and two bathrooms in both. Their layouts and listed space requirements differ, so the lower model price is only one part of the choice.

    3.4

    Lead with a concrete trade-off.

    L-795 starts $20k below L-800 while keeping two bedrooms. I put that difference before the cards, then align floor plans and space requirements so price has context.

  4. L-795 is worth exploring for your cost priority while keeping two bedrooms.

    Starting prices exclude installation. A lot review is needed to confirm property fit and the complete project budget.

    3.5

    Make the limit part of the recommendation.

    The starting price excludes installation, and property fit still needs review. I placed that qualification beside the lot-review action so the next step follows the unanswered question.

04 Compare and verify

What comes after the shortlist?

A lower starting price can make a model worth considering, but it cannot tell a visitor whether that home will work on their property. I designed this transition around the questions still unanswered: keep the comparison available, open LiveLarge’s existing lot review alongside the conversation, and make clear that the chat is not sent with the form.

05 Visual design

An AI presence
that feels at home.

LiveLarge wanted the assistant to feel recognizably intelligent within a website led by its homes. I translated that brief into two expressive moments: the first greeting and the wait for a reply. In the working conversation, familiar typography and quiet surfaces let the model, the guidance and the next action take priority.

Give each visual element a job.

I kept the assistant in LiveLarge’s Barlow typography and neutral interface language. Color introduces it; a brief edge cue acknowledges a pending response. The message area settles back to a plain reading surface.

5.2 · Response state

Keep motion at the edge.

I wanted the assistant to feel responsive while keeping the conversation easy to read. I used a moving light along the panel edge while a reply is being prepared, then let it fade as the answer appears.

06 From design to working behavior

Make the design rules
visible in the prototype.

I translated the interaction design into briefs for coding agents, then checked the working prototype against the design. I used screenshots and recorded walkthroughs to inspect page context, conversation state and comparison layouts across desktop and mobile. These checks made the behavior concrete: what a new conversation clears, what minimizing preserves, and how much space a comparison needs.

A new conversation is still on the same page.

I explicitly separated conversation state from page context. Starting over clears the visitor’s previous messages and needs, but the assistant should still recognize the model currently on screen.

Conversation starts freshCurrent page stays in context

Hiding the window should preserve the work.

I specified that minimize only changes visibility. Reopening restores the thread, while comparison gets enough width to keep its content readable. These became concrete checks for the working build.
